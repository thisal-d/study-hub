## 📌 Part 1: Conceptual & Theoretical Solutions

### Solution 1
**Correct Option: B** (External stakeholders like investors, lenders, regulators, and suppliers)
* **Explanation:** Financial accounting focuses on producing general-purpose financial reports (Balance Sheet, Income Statement, Cash Flow Statement) for external decision-makers to evaluate stability, profitability, and solvency. Internal operational tracking (e.g., project hours, sprint velocity) is handled by cost/managerial accounting.

---

### Solution 2
1. **Expense vs. Expenditure:**
   - **Expense:** Everyday operating costs consumed within the current accounting period to support ongoing operations (e.g., engineer monthly salaries, monthly cloud hosting subscriptions, office electricity).
   - **Expenditure:** Any outflow of money. Not all expenditures are expenses—for example, spending LKR 2,500,000 to purchase a high-performance database server cluster is a **Capital Expenditure (CapEx)**, which is recorded on the Balance Sheet as a **Fixed Asset**, not an immediate operational expense.
2. **Net Profit vs. Operating Cash Flow:**
   - Financial accounting uses **accrual accounting**, recognizing revenue when services are delivered, even if payment is pending (Accounts Receivable).
   - If an IT startup delivers LKR 5,000,000 worth of enterprise software on 90-day credit, it reports a profit on the Income Statement. However, if clients have not yet paid and the startup has zero cash in the bank to pay developer salaries, rent, and cloud hosting bills, it will suffer a liquidity crisis and face **bankruptcy**.

---

### Solution 3
* **1 $\rightarrow$ C:** Balance Sheet $\rightarrow$ Static snapshot of assets, liabilities, and owner's worth at a specific point in time.
* **2 $\rightarrow$ B:** Income Statement (P&L) $\rightarrow$ Measures profitability and operational performance over a period of time.
* **3 $\rightarrow$ A:** Cash Flow Statement $\rightarrow$ Shows cash inflows and outflows across Operating, Investing, and Financing activities.

## 📌 Part 3: Income Statement Insights & Profit Levels Solutions

### Solution 5 (Key Insights from the Income Statement)
1. **Dimension: Cost Control & Operational Efficiency**
   - When revenue increases (+30%) but net profit drops (-15%), it signals poor cost control—operating expenses or cost of sales grew at a much faster rate than sales. Management must audit escalating cloud hosting fees, administrative overheads, or project delivery overruns.
2. **Operating Profit vs. Net Profit:**
   - **Operating Profit (EBIT)** reflects pure performance from core IT activities (software sales minus direct costs and overheads), isolating business strength from external variables like debt interest rates (financing structure) and government corporate tax rates.
3. **Trends Over Time:**
   - Comparing statements across consecutive years reveals growth trajectory, revenue scalability, seasonal demand shifts, and whether cost reduction strategies are working sustainably.

---

### Solution 6 (Profit Levels & Formulas)
1. **Gross Profit:**
   $$\text{Gross Profit} = \text{Revenue} - \text{Cost of Sales}$$
   *Meaning:* Measures core production and service delivery efficiency before overhead costs.
2. **Operating Profit (EBIT):**
   $$\text{Operating Profit} = \text{Gross Profit} - \text{Operating Expenses}$$
   *Meaning:* Measures profitability generated strictly from day-to-day core business operations.
3. **Net Profit:**
   $$\text{Net Profit} = \text{Operating Profit} - (\text{Finance Costs} + \text{Taxes})$$
   *Meaning:* The final "bottom line" residual profit available to the business owners and shareholders.

## 📌 Part 4: Practical Cash Flow Management Solutions

### Solution 7 (Why Cash Flow Matters)
1. **Ensuring Liquidity:** Guarantees sufficient cash on hand to pay monthly salaries, server bills, and vendor obligations without default.
2. **Supporting Business Survival:** Cash is the lifeblood of operations; even profitable companies fail if cash is depleted.
3. **Indicating Financial Health:** Positive operating cash flow proves the company's business model is self-sustaining rather than dependent on constant borrowing.
4. **Improving Decision-Making:** Helps IT leadership schedule major capital investments (e.g., data center upgrades, platform rewrites) based on actual cash generation capacity.

## 📌 Part 6: Income Statement Solutions

### Solution 11 (Multi-Tier Income Statement)
1. **Gross Profit:**
   $$\text{Gross Profit} = 800,000 - 360,000 = \mathbf{LKR\ 440,000}$$
2. **Operating Profit (EBIT):**
   $$\text{Operating Profit} = 440,000 - 240,000 = \mathbf{LKR\ 200,000}$$
3. **PBT & Net Profit:**
   $$\text{Profit Before Tax (PBT)} = 200,000 - 30,000 = \text{LKR } 170,000$$
   $$\text{Net Profit} = 170,000 - 42,500 = \mathbf{LKR\ 127,500}$$
4. **Profit Margins:**
   $$\text{Gross Profit Margin} = \left(\frac{440,000}{800,000}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{55\%}$$
   $$\text{Net Profit Margin} = \left(\frac{127,500}{800,000}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{15.94\%}$$
5. **Shareholders' Equity:**
   $$\text{Equity} = \text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities} = 1,200,000 - 500,000 = \mathbf{LKR\ 700,000}$$
6. **Practical Interpretation:**
   An Operating Profit of LKR 200,000 (25% operating margin) indicates solid core operational efficiency; the company's daily IT operations are highly viable and easily generate enough cash to service bank interest and taxes.

## 📌 Part 7: Cash Flow Solutions

### Solution 12 (Net Cash Flow Calculations & Liquidity Evaluation)
1. **Operating Cash Flow ($CFO$):**
   $$CFO = +650,000 (\text{Customer receipts}) - 380,000 (\text{Operating expenses}) = \mathbf{+LKR\ 270,000}$$
2. **Investing Cash Flow ($CFI$):**
   $$CFI = +25,000 (\text{Hardware disposal}) - 180,000 (\text{Equipment purchase}) = \mathbf{-LKR\ 155,000}$$
3. **Financing Cash Flow ($CFF$):**
   $$CFF = +200,000 (\text{Loan received}) - 75,000 (\text{Loan repaid}) - 45,000 (\text{Dividends}) = \mathbf{+LKR\ 80,000}$$
4. **Net Increase in Cash:**
   $$\text{Net Cash Flow} = +270,000 - 155,000 + 80,000 = \mathbf{+LKR\ 195,000}$$
5. **Practical Liquidity Assessment:**
   Yes, Vortex Systems' cash position is **strongly improving**. The company's core operations generate a positive cash surplus ($+270,000$) that comfortably covers its capital reinvestment in technology infrastructure ($-155,000$) and enables regular debt servicing and dividend payouts, leaving a net cash addition of LKR 195,000.

## 📌 Part 8: Financial Ratio Analysis Solutions

### Solution 13 (Telecom Case Study Analysis)
1. **Gross Profit Margin:**
   $$\text{Gross Profit} = 240 - 120 = \text{LKR } 120\text{ Billion}$$
   $$\text{Gross Profit Margin} = \left(\frac{120}{240}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{50\%}$$
2. **Net Profit Margin:**
   $$\text{Net Profit Margin} = \left(\frac{33}{240}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{13.75\%}$$
3. **Current Ratio:**
   $$\text{Current Ratio} = \frac{90}{45} = \mathbf{2:1} \quad (2.0)$$
   *Interpretation:* Strong short-term liquidity. Holds LKR 2 in liquid current assets for every LKR 1 in upcoming obligations.
4. **Debt-to-Equity Ratio:**
   $$\text{Debt-to-Equity} = \frac{270}{90} = \mathbf{3.0:1}$$
5. **Managerial Evaluation:**
   - **Working Capital Efficiency:** A fast receivables collection cycle (customers paying upfront via prepaid recharges) combined with moderate payables credit terms means the company receives cash immediately from users before having to pay its equipment vendors. This creates a positive working capital cycle and boosts liquidity.
   - **High Gearing Justification in Telecom:** Telecom networks require massive upfront capital expenditures (cell towers, fiber optic backbones, spectrum licenses). Borrowing debt allows nationwide expansion without diluting equity ownership. Because subscriber demand is stable and recurring monthly cash inflows are predictable, the company can safely service this high debt burden.
